# Problem 19: Counting Sundays

https://projecteuler.net/problem=19

**Answer:** 171 · **Solution:** `P19.java` · **Runtime:** < 0.01 s

## Problem

You are given the following information, but you may prefer to do some research for yourself.

- 1 Jan 1900 was a Monday.
- Thirty days has September, April, June and November. All the rest have thirty-one, saving February alone,
  which has twenty-eight, rain or shine. And on leap years, twenty-nine.
- A leap year occurs on any year evenly divisible by 4, but not on a century unless it is divisible by 400.

How many Sundays fell on the first of the month during the twentieth century (1 Jan 1901 to 31 Dec 2000)?

## In Plain Terms

A calendar year only comes in 14 flavours: it can start on any of the 7 weekdays, and be a leap year or not.
For each flavour, count once how many months begin on a Sunday. Then walk through 1901–2000, track which
weekday each year starts on, and add up the matching counts.

## In Theory

Days of the week work **modulo 7**. If a month starts on weekday d and has m days, the next month starts on
(d + m) mod 7. So for each starting weekday s and year type, the twelve month-start weekdays are fixed, and
we can precompute

```
sundays[type][s] = number of months starting on Sunday, given Jan 1 falls on weekday s
```

The next year starts on (s + 365) mod 7 or (s + 366) mod 7. In 1901–2000 the leap years are exactly the
years divisible by 4 (2000 is divisible by 400, so it counts), which keeps the leap-year test simple.

1 Jan 1901 = Monday + 365 days = Tuesday.

## In Code

1. For each Jan-1 weekday `jan_1` (0 = Sunday … 6 = Saturday), step through the month lengths with
   `add_month`, which advances the weekday and counts a Sunday whenever it lands on 0. This fills
   `regular_year_sundays` and `leap_year_sundays`.
2. Start at Tuesday for 1901; for each year add the right table entry and advance by 365 or 366 days.

## Complexity

O(years) after a constant-size precomputation (7 × 12 × 2 steps). Space O(1).

## Further Thoughts

The original version didn't reset the running weekday at the start of each `jan_1` iteration, so each table
row was computed from the previous row's 1 December instead of from `jan_1`. The tables came out scrambled,
but the 100-year total happened to still be 171. Resetting the weekday to `jan_1` fixed the tables.

`java.time.LocalDate` makes the brute-force version a one-liner loop over 1,200 months.
