# Problem 65: Convergents of e

https://projecteuler.net/problem=65

**Answer:** 272 · **Solution:** `P65.java` · **Runtime:** < 0.01 s

## Problem

The square root of 2 can be written as an infinite continued fraction.

```
√2 = 1 + 1/(2 + 1/(2 + 1/(2 + ...)))
```

The infinite continued fraction can be written, √2 = [1; (2)], where (2) indicates that 2 repeats ad infinitum.
In a similar way, √23 = [4; (1, 3, 1, 8)].

It turns out that the sequence of partial values of continued fractions for square roots provide the best
rational approximations. The sequence of the first ten convergents for √2 are:

```
1, 3/2, 7/5, 17/12, 41/29, 99/70, 239/169, 577/408, 1393/985, 3363/2378, ...
```

What is most surprising is that the important mathematical constant,

```
e = [2; 1, 2, 1, 1, 4, 1, 1, 6, 1, ... , 1, 2k, 1, ...]
```

The first ten terms in the sequence of convergents for e are:

```
2, 3, 8/3, 11/4, 19/7, 87/32, 106/39, 193/71, 1264/465, 1457/536, ...
```

The sum of digits in the numerator of the 10th convergent is 1 + 4 + 5 + 7 = 17.

Find the sum of digits in the numerator of the 100th convergent of the continued fraction for e.

## In Plain Terms

The number e can be approximated by a sequence of fractions that keep getting better, and there's a simple
recipe to get each fraction's top number from the previous two. We follow the recipe 100 times — the numbers
grow to about 58 digits — then add up the digits of the final top number.

## In Theory

For a continued fraction [a₀; a₁, a₂, …], numerators satisfy

```
h₋₁ = 1,   h₀ = a₀,   hₖ = aₖ·hₖ₋₁ + hₖ₋₂
```

For e, the terms are aₖ = 2(k+1)/3 when k ≡ 2 (mod 3), and 1 otherwise (for k ≥ 1). Only numerators are needed.

The 100th numerator is 6963524437876961749120273824619538346438023188214475670667, whose digits sum to 272.

## In Code

1. Start with `prev_n = 2` (h₀) and `n = 3` (h₁).
2. For `i` from 2 to 99: when `i % 3 == 2` the term is the next even number (`factor += 2`), otherwise 1;
   update `n = term × n + prev_n` with `BigInteger`.
3. Sum the digits of `n`.

## Complexity

O(k × d) for k terms with d-digit numbers. Space O(d).
