# Problem 58: Spiral Primes

https://projecteuler.net/problem=58

**Answer:** 26241 · **Solution:** `P58.java` · **Runtime:** ~0.09 s

## Problem

Starting with 1 and spiralling anticlockwise in the following way, a square spiral with side length 7 is formed.

```
37 36 35 34 33 32 31
38 17 16 15 14 13 30
39 18  5  4  3 12 29
40 19  6  1  2 11 28
41 20  7  8  9 10 27
42 21 22 23 24 25 26
43 44 45 46 47 48 49
```

It is interesting to note that the odd squares lie along the bottom right diagonal, but what is more interesting
is that 8 out of the 13 numbers lying along both diagonals are prime; that is, a ratio of 8/13 ≈ 62%.

If one complete new layer is wrapped around the spiral above, a square spiral with side length 9 will be formed.
If this process is continued, what is the side length of the square spiral for which the ratio of primes along
both diagonals first falls below 10%?

## In Plain Terms

Like Problem 28, each new ring of the spiral adds four corner numbers that follow a simple pattern. Add rings
one at a time, check which of the new corners are prime, and keep a running fraction of primes on the
diagonals. Primes get rarer as numbers grow, so the fraction slowly falls. Stop when it drops below 10%.

## In Theory

For the ring with side length s (odd), the four corners are

```
s²,  s² − (s−1),  s² − 2(s−1),  s² − 3(s−1)
```

The bottom-right corner s² is a perfect square and never prime, so only three corners need testing. The
diagonals of an s×s spiral contain 2s − 1 numbers in total.

By the prime number theorem, a number near N is prime with probability about 1/ln N, so the ratio declines
slowly — the 10% mark isn't reached until side length 26,241, where corner values approach 6.9 × 10⁸.

## In Code

1. Start from the 3×3 spiral (3 primes out of 5 diagonal numbers) and track the three non-square corners
   `ne`, `nw`, `sw`.
2. For each new ring, advance each corner by the growing gap `addon`, test the three with `Primes.isPrime`,
   and add 4 to the diagonal count.
3. Stop when `primes / nums < 0.1` and return the side length.

## Complexity

About s/2 rings, each with three primality tests costing O(√(s²)) = O(s) — roughly O(s²) overall. Space O(1).

## Further Thoughts

For much larger cutoffs, a deterministic **Miller–Rabin** test makes each primality check O(log³ N) instead of
O(√N).
