# Problem 51: Prime Digit Replacements

https://projecteuler.net/problem=51

**Answer:** 121313 · **Solution:** `P51.java` · **Runtime:** ~1.5 s

## Problem

By replacing the 1st digit of the 2-digit number \*3, it turns out that six of the nine possible values: 13, 23,
43, 53, 73, and 83, are all prime.

By replacing the 3rd and 4th digits of 56\*\*3 with the same digit, this 5-digit number is the first example
having seven primes among the ten generated numbers, yielding the family: 56003, 56113, 56333, 56443, 56663,
56773, and 56993. Consequently 56003, being the first member of this family, is the smallest prime with this
property.

Find the smallest prime which, by replacing part of the number (not necessarily adjacent digits) with the same
digit, is part of an eight prime value family.

## In Plain Terms

Take a number and blank out some of its digits, like 56\*\*3. Now fill every blank with the same digit, 0 through
9, giving ten numbers. We want a pattern where at least eight of those ten are prime, and the answer is the
smallest prime in that family. So the search is over patterns: how long the number is, which positions are
blanks, and what the fixed digits are. The winning pattern is \*2\*3\*3, which gives 121313, 222323, 323333,
424343, 525353, 626363, 828383 and 929393 — eight primes.

## In Theory

Two facts narrow the search a lot:

- **The last digit can't be a blank.** Filling it with 0, 2, 4, 5, 6 or 8 gives six non-primes, leaving at most four.
- **The number of blanks must be a multiple of 3.** Each blank digit d adds d × (sum of the blanks' place values),
  and every place value is ≡ 1 (mod 3). If the blank count k isn't a multiple of 3, then as d runs through 0–9
  the numbers' remainders mod 3 cycle through all three values, so at least three of the ten are divisible by 3 —
  leaving at most seven primes.

So the smallest candidates are 6-digit numbers with 3 blanks and a last digit of 1, 3, 7 or 9.

## In Code

1. For each length `i` (2, 3, …) and blank count `j`, build every multiset of `i − j` fixed digits as a
   non-decreasing string (`digitMultisets`, e.g. "233"), then append `j` asterisks.
2. `Lexography.sortedPermutations` places the fixed digits and blanks in every distinct arrangement.
3. Skip patterns ending in `*`, an even digit or 5, or starting with 0.
4. Replace every `*` with 0–9 (skipping 0 when the pattern starts with `*`) and count primes with
   `Primes.isPrime`. The first pattern with at least 8 primes gives the answer: its smallest member.

## Complexity

Dominated by the 6-digit case: for each blank count, C(10 + r − 1, r) digit multisets for r fixed digits, times
the arrangements of each, times 10 primality checks of O(√n). Roughly a million primality tests in all.
Space O(number of multisets).

## Further Thoughts

- **Two bugs fixed while finishing this one.** `Lexography.sortedPermutations` dropped arrangements when a
  character repeated ("1233" gave 9 of 12; "233\*\*\*" gave 50 of 60 and missed \*2\*3\*3), because `findCeil`
  picked the leftmost of several equal characters instead of the rightmost. And choosing the fixed digits with
  `getCombinations` over a 10·r-character array walked about 2 million combinations to find 2,002 distinct digit
  sets; generating the multisets directly brought the runtime from ~80 s to ~1.5 s.
- Skipping blank counts that aren't multiples of 3 (and never blanking the last digit) would cut the search much
  further, using the facts above.
- The search returns the first family it meets, in sorted-string order — not strictly the family with the
  smallest prime among all 6-digit families. It's right here (121313 is the known answer), but a fully careful
  version would finish the length-6 search and take the minimum.
