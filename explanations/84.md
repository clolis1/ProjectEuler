# Problem 84: Monopoly Odds

https://projecteuler.net/problem=84

**Answer:** 101524 · **Solution:** `p084.py` · **Runtime:** ~0.01 s

## Problem

In the game, Monopoly, the standard board is set up in the following way:

```
GO   A1   CC1  A2   T1   R1   B1   CH1  B2   B3   JAIL
H2                                                C1
T2                                                U1
H1                                                C2
CH3                                               C3
R4                                                R2
G3                                                D1
CC3                                               CC2
G2                                                D2
G1                                                D3
G2J  F3   U2   F2   F1   R3   E3   E2   CH2  E1   FP
```

A player starts on the GO square and adds the scores on two 6-sided dice to determine the number of squares
they advance in a clockwise direction. Without any further rules we would expect to visit each square with
equal probability: 2.5%. However, landing on G2J (Go To Jail), CC (community chest), and CH (chance) changes
this distribution.

In addition to G2J, and one card from each of CC and CH, that orders the player to go directly to jail, if a
player rolls three consecutive doubles, they do not advance the result of their 3rd roll. Instead they proceed
directly to jail.

At the beginning of the game, the CC and CH cards are shuffled. When a player lands on CC or CH they take a
card from the top of the respective pile and, after following the instructions, it is returned to the bottom of
the pile. There are sixteen cards in each pile, but for the purpose of this problem we are only concerned with
cards that order a movement; any instruction not concerned with movement will be ignored and the player will
remain on the CC/CH square.

- Community Chest (2/16 cards):
  1. Advance to GO
  2. Go to JAIL
- Chance (10/16 cards):
  1. Advance to GO
  2. Go to JAIL
  3. Go to C1
  4. Go to E3
  5. Go to H2
  6. Go to R1
  7. Go to next R (railway company)
  8. Go to next R
  9. Go to next U (utility company)
  10. Go back 3 squares.

The heart of this problem concerns the likelihood of visiting a particular square. That is, the probability of
finishing at that square after a roll. For this reason it should be clear that, with the exception of G2J for
which the probability of finishing on it is zero, the CH squares will have the lowest probabilities, as 5/8
request a movement to another square, and it is the final square that the player finishes at on each roll that
we are interested in. We shall make no distinction between "Just Visiting" and being sent to JAIL, and we shall
also ignore the rule about requiring a double to "get out of jail", assuming that they pay to get out on their
next turn.

By starting at GO and numbering the squares sequentially from 00 to 39 we can concatenate these two-digit
numbers to produce strings that correspond with sets of squares.

Statistically it can be shown that the three most popular squares, in order, are JAIL (6.24%) = Square 10,
E3 (3.18%) = Square 24, and GO (3.09%) = Square 00. So these three most popular squares can be listed with the
six-digit modal string: 102400.

If, instead of using two 6-sided dice, two 4-sided dice are used, find the six-digit modal string.

## In Plain Terms

Roll two dice, move that many squares, and some squares send you somewhere else: Go To Jail, the Chance and
Community Chest cards, and rolling three doubles in a row. The question is which squares you end up on most often
over a long game.

The slow way is to play millions of turns and count. Instead, I wrote down a table of "if you start a turn here,
here's the chance you end up there" for every square. Every turn is just that table applied again. Then there's a
single set of percentages that the table leaves unchanged: if that's how likely you are to be on each square
before a turn, it's still exactly how likely you are after it. That balance point is the long-run answer, and
linear algebra can find it directly instead of waiting for millions of turns to settle there.

With 4-sided dice, the three most visited squares are JAIL (about 7%), R2 and E3.

## In Theory

**The game is a Markov chain.** Where you go next depends only on where you are now, so one turn is a
**transition matrix** M, where M[i][j] is the probability that a turn starting in state i ends in state j. Each row
adds to 1, because you always end up somewhere. If v holds the probability of being in each state, one turn is
v · M.

**Building M.** Start from the dice alone. Two 4-sided dice give 16 equally likely rolls, so totals from 2 to 8
come up 1, 2, 3, 4, 3, 2, 1 times out of 16. Then apply each rule by moving probability around within each row,
never creating or losing any:

- **Go To Jail**: whatever lands on G2J moves to JAIL.
- **Chance**: each of the 10 movement cards takes 1/16 of what lands on a CH square to its destination; 6/16 stays.
  "Next R", "next U" and "back 3" depend on which CH square you're on.
- **Community Chest**: 1/16 to GO, 1/16 to JAIL, 14/16 stays.

**Order matters.** A rule only reshuffles the probability already sitting on its own squares. "Back 3" from CH3 lands
on CC3, and you draw a Community Chest card there, so Chance has to be applied before Community Chest.

**The three-doubles rule needs more than 40 states.** A square alone doesn't tell you how many doubles you've just
rolled. So a state is (square, streak), with streak 0, 1 or 2, which makes 40 × 3 = **120 states**. A double moves you
and raises the streak; a non-double resets it to 0; a double on streak 2 sends you to (JAIL, 0) instead of moving.
Every way of being sent to jail resets the streak.

**The answer is an eigenvector.** Adding up a column of M isn't the answer, because that treats every starting state
as equally likely, and they aren't. What we want is the distribution π that one more turn doesn't change:

```
π · M = π
```

That's an **eigenvector** of M with **eigenvalue 1**. Every transition matrix has eigenvalue 1, because its rows add
to 1. The other eigenvalues are smaller, which is why repeatedly playing turns (or multiplying by M) converges to π.
Since the eigenvalue is known to be 1, π can also be found directly by solving the linear equations
π · (M − I) = 0 with the entries adding to 1.

Finally, add each square's three streak values together, sort, and take the top three: 10 (JAIL), 15 (R2),
24 (E3) → **101524**.

**Check:** the same model with 6-sided dice gives 102400, matching the statement (JAIL 6.22%, E3 3.19%, GO 3.10%,
against the statement's 6.24%, 3.18% and 3.09%).

## In Code

`p084.py` reads top to bottom: the answer, the matrix, then the rules.

1. `modal_string` is the whole method in one place: build the dice matrix, apply the rules in order
   (`go_to_jail`, `chance`, `community_chest`; chance before community chest so "back 3" onto CC3 draws a card),
   check every row still adds to 1, find the eigenvector, add up each square's three streaks, and take the top three.
2. `dice_matrix` builds the matrix as a 4-D array, `M[from_square, from_streak, to_square, to_streak]`, from every
   pair of dice faces: a double raises the streak, a non-double resets it, and a third double goes to (JAIL, 0).
   It's only flattened to 120×120 for the eigenvector.
3. The rules live at the bottom. Each one just lists where probability goes:
   `go_to_jail` sends all of G2J to JAIL, `community_chest` sends 1/16 to GO and 1/16 to JAIL, and `chance` sends
   1/16 to each of its ten destinations, working out "next R", "next U" and "back 3" from the board.
4. `redirect` does the actual moving for all three rules: it takes what lands on a square, moves each fraction to
   its destination keeping your streak (except into jail, which resets it), and leaves the rest where it is.
5. The eigenvector: `np.linalg.eig` on the **transpose** of the flattened matrix (eig solves M · x = λx with the
   vector on the right; we want π · M = π with it on the left), keeping the eigenvector whose eigenvalue is 1.

## Complexity

Building the matrix is O(S × n²) for S = 120 states and n-sided dice. The eigen-decomposition is O(S³), about
1.7 million operations for S = 120. The whole thing runs in about 0.01 s.

## Further Thoughts

- **Power iteration** (start anywhere and multiply by M until v stops changing) reaches the same π. Its error shrinks
  by the second-largest eigenvalue (about 0.86 here) each pass, so it takes roughly 100 passes to get six-digit
  accuracy. It's the method PageRank uses on matrices far too big for eig.
- The statement's percentages are slightly different from this model's. They were probably estimated by simulation,
  or with a slightly different reading of the rules. The top-three order is the same either way.
- The model treats the card piles as a random draw each time. Real piles are shuffled once and cycled in order, but
  over a long game every card comes up equally often, so the long-run probabilities are the same.
